Yu-Gi-Oh! Card Game History

A historical encyclopedia documenting the development of the Yu-Gi-Oh! card game from Kazuki Takahashi's original manga concept, the earliest Japanese card products and Konami's 1999 Official Card Game, through the international Trading Card Game, competitive formats, summoning systems, booster sets, special products, tournament history, World Championships, digital adaptations, collecting culture, and the modern game.

The Historical Record of the Yu-Gi-Oh! Card Game

Yu-Gi-Oh! is unusual among major trading-card games because its fictional version existed before the modern physical game that millions of players eventually came to know. The card battles that appeared in Kazuki Takahashi's manga developed from one of many different games played by protagonist Yugi Mutou into the central identity of an international franchise. The fictional game was originally known as Magic & Wizards in the manga and later became known as Duel Monsters. As reader interest in the card battles grew, physical interpretations appeared, and Konami ultimately established the Official Card Game in Japan.

From that beginning the game developed into two closely related organized card-game systems: the Official Card Game (OCG), primarily used in Japan and numerous Asian territories, and the Trading Card Game (TCG), used in regions including North America, Latin America and Europe. The two versions share the same basic game and enormous portions of the same card pool, while release schedules, card availability, product structures and Forbidden & Limited Lists can differ.

Over more than two decades, Yu-Gi-Oh! evolved from a relatively direct battle game centered heavily on Normal Summons, Tribute Summons, battle statistics and individually powerful cards into a game built around interconnected archetypes, graveyard resources, elaborate effect chains and multiple Extra Deck summoning systems. Fusion Monsters, Ritual Monsters, Synchro Monsters, Xyz Monsters, Pendulum Monsters and Link Monsters each represent a major layer of the game's mechanical history.

This page treats Yu-Gi-Oh! as both a game and a historical record. It therefore documents not only rules and cards, but also product eras, terminology, competitive strategy, tournament structures, collecting, promotional cards, major rule changes and the changing ways players have experienced the game.

About the complete card catalog: Yu-Gi-Oh! contains many thousands of individual cards and continues receiving new releases. Rather than freezing an enormous card list that would immediately become outdated, this archive links directly to Konami's official Yu-Gi-Oh! Neuron Card Database. The database allows every currently cataloged card to be searched by name, text, card type, Attribute, Type, Level, Rank, Pendulum Scale, Link Rating, ATK, DEF and product appearance.

1. Origins — Kazuki Takahashi, the Manga, and the Fictional Card Game

The history of the Yu-Gi-Oh! card game begins before the physical Konami game existed. Kazuki Takahashi created Yu-Gi-Oh! as a manga centered on games, puzzles, psychology and high-stakes confrontations. The manga began serialization in Shueisha's Weekly Shonen Jump in 1996.

Early Yu-Gi-Oh! stories were not exclusively about cards. Yugi and the other characters encountered many kinds of games. Card battling was simply one concept among them. The fictional card game initially appeared under the name Magic & Wizards, a name that acknowledged the influence of real tabletop card games while establishing a fictional game with its own monsters, spells and dueling culture.

Reader interest in the card-game chapters helped move the series increasingly toward Duel Monsters. Characters such as Seto Kaiba, Maximillion Pegasus, Joey Wheeler, Yami Yugi and others became closely associated with signature monsters and strategies. Blue-Eyes White Dragon, Dark Magician, Red-Eyes Black Dragon and Exodia became cultural icons before many international players ever handled a physical Yu-Gi-Oh! card.

Manga Origin

Yu-Gi-Oh! Begins

The original manga began in 1996. Its title can be translated roughly as "King of Games," an idea that fit the early series because Yugi encountered many different forms of competition.

Fictional Game

Magic & Wizards / Duel Monsters

The fictional card game gradually became the dominant game within the franchise. Anime adaptations popularized the name Duel Monsters internationally.

Central Idea

Monsters, Magic and Traps

The recognizable structure of monsters fighting while players use supportive magical and reactive cards formed the conceptual basis of the later physical game.

Why the Manga Matters to Card-Game History

In many card-game franchises, fiction is created to support a game that already exists. Yu-Gi-Oh! developed in the opposite direction. The dramatic duels, named monsters and imagined rules came first, and real card products were developed around a fictional game audiences had already seen characters play.

This helps explain why the earliest Yu-Gi-Oh! cards sometimes appear unusual when judged only from competitive design. Many cards were attempting to recreate monsters, magical objects and dramatic effects associated with manga and anime scenes. Over time, the physical game developed an increasingly independent rules identity.

The tension between recreating fictional duels and maintaining a balanced competitive game has remained part of Yu-Gi-Oh!'s identity throughout its history. Modern products continue to reinterpret old anime and manga strategies using effects designed for contemporary play.

Early Physical Card Products Before Konami's Modern OCG

Yu-Gi-Oh! merchandise appeared during the franchise's early growth, including earlier physical card interpretations. These should not automatically be treated as identical to the modern Konami OCG/TCG lineage. The modern competitive card game is generally traced to Konami's Japanese Official Card Game launched in 1999.

For collectors and historians, distinguishing early franchise cards from the later standardized Konami game is important. Similar characters and monsters can appear across products that were never mutually compatible as tournament cards.

2. 1999 — Birth of Konami's Yu-Gi-Oh! Official Card Game

Konami's Yu-Gi-Oh! Official Card Game established the direct lineage of the physical game played today. The earliest OCG releases appeared in Japan in 1999 and created the framework from which the later international TCG developed.

These early sets differ dramatically from modern releases. Card design was simpler, monsters were often judged primarily by ATK and DEF statistics, archetype-based deck building had not yet become the dominant design philosophy, and the modern Extra Deck ecosystem did not exist.

Early Tournament and Promotional Cards

Tournament participation cards, prize cards, magazine promotions, guidebook cards, video-game promotional cards and event-exclusive cards became important almost immediately. This distribution model would become a recurring feature of Yu-Gi-Oh! collecting.

Some early cards obtained historical significance because their distribution was extremely limited. Tournament prize cards in particular became symbols of competitive achievement as well as highly specialized collector items.

OCG and TCG Are Related but Not Identical

OCG and TCG players use the same fundamental rules and share most card designs, but their release schedules and legal card pools can differ. A card may become available in Japan before receiving a TCG release.

Their Forbidden & Limited Lists can also differ. This is why a competitive deck considered legal or dominant in one environment cannot always be assumed to be identical in the other.

World Championship events require special handling because competitors arrive from regions governed by different card pools and restriction lists. Historically, World Championship rules have used specially defined legality requirements to create a common tournament environment.

3. 2002 — International Yu-Gi-Oh! Trading Card Game Expansion

The early 2000s transformed Yu-Gi-Oh! from a Japanese franchise with an established card game into an international phenomenon. The anime's international success created a huge audience for the physical cards. In North America, the recognizable English TCG booster era began in 2002.

For a generation of players, the arrival of Legend of Blue-Eyes White Dragon, Starter Deck: Yugi and Starter Deck: Kaiba represented their first opportunity to recreate the television duels using official cards. Schoolyards, hobby shops, toy stores, local tournaments and organized competitive events rapidly developed around the game.

The Foundational TCG Sets

Legend of Blue-Eyes White Dragon

The first TCG booster established many of the game's most recognizable early cards and introduced international players to the booster-pack model.

It contained iconic monsters including Blue-Eyes White Dragon while also establishing early staples, Normal Monsters, Fusions, Spells and Traps.

Metal Raiders

The second major TCG booster expanded effect-monster design and introduced cards that became long-term landmarks of early competitive Yu-Gi-Oh!.

Magic Ruler / Spell Ruler

This set dramatically expanded Spell design and introduced Ritual Monsters, Ritual Spells, Quick-Play Spells, Toon cards and several historically powerful utility cards.

Pharaoh's Servant

Pharaoh's Servant continued the rapid growth of the early card pool and included cards that became closely associated with competitive control and disruption.

Why the Early TCG Felt Different From Modern Yu-Gi-Oh!

Early players frequently evaluated monsters by whether their raw ATK justified a Normal Summon or Tribute Summon. Dedicated archetype engines existed only in limited forms. Decks were more likely to combine individually powerful cards than to operate as tightly connected engines of cards sharing a name.

Card advantage was particularly visible. Effects that removed multiple opponent cards, drew additional cards or stripped cards from the opponent's hand could dominate because replacing lost resources was more difficult than in many modern strategies.

Modern decks can frequently use the Graveyard as an extension of the hand, Special Summon repeatedly, search specific combo pieces and produce several disruptions during a single turn. The earliest TCG environment generally operated at a slower resource pace.

The Importance of Starter Deck: Yugi and Starter Deck: Kaiba

The Yugi and Kaiba Starter Decks linked the real game directly to the anime rivalry. Players could own recognizable cards associated with Yugi Mutou and Seto Kaiba rather than beginning entirely with random booster packs.

Character-linked products became a permanent Yu-Gi-Oh! marketing tradition. Later Starter Decks, Structure Decks, Legendary Decks, Duelist Packs and nostalgia products repeatedly returned to characters and signature strategies from the manga and anime.

4. Historical Timeline — 1996 Through 2026

1996 — Yu-Gi-Oh! Manga Begins

Kazuki Takahashi's manga begins serialization and introduces Yugi Mutou, the Millennium Puzzle and a story built around games and psychological contests.

1998 — Early Yu-Gi-Oh! Video Games and Merchandise

Yu-Gi-Oh! expands beyond manga into games and merchandise. Konami releases early Yu-Gi-Oh! video-game material in Japan, helping establish its future role as the central game company behind the franchise.

1999 — Konami Official Card Game

The Japanese OCG establishes the modern physical card-game lineage. Early sets include Vol. releases, Booster products, Starter Boxes, Premium Packs and tournament promotions.

2000–2001 — OCG Expansion

The Japanese card pool rapidly grows. Product design, terminology, monster effects, Fusion mechanics and competitive concepts develop before the international TCG launch.

2002 — International TCG Explosion

Legend of Blue-Eyes White Dragon, Metal Raiders, Magic Ruler/Spell Ruler and Pharaoh's Servant establish the earliest English-language booster era. Starter Decks based on Yugi and Kaiba help the card game reach a mass audience.

2003 — First Physical-Card World Championship

The first Yu-Gi-Oh! World Championship for the physical OCG/TCG is held in New York City. Yu Leung Ng becomes the inaugural World Champion.

2004 — First Forbidden Cards

Competitive balance evolves from restriction-only approaches toward a formal Forbidden category. Chaos strategies become defining examples of powerful early deck construction.

2005 — Goat-Era Yu-Gi-Oh! and Cyber Dragon

The 2005 competitive environment later becomes one of the most widely replayed historical formats. Cybernetic Revolution and cards such as Cyber Dragon symbolize a transition toward faster monster deployment and more specialized themes.

2006–2007 — Monarchs, Machines and GX-Era Archetypes

Tribute-based Monarch strategies, Cyber Dragon engines, Destiny HERO cards, Gadgets and increasingly coherent themes demonstrate how the game's design is becoming more synergistic.

2008 — Synchro Summoning Changes the Extra Deck

The Duelist Genesis introduces the TCG to Synchro Monsters and Tuners. The former Fusion Deck has evolved into a broader Extra Deck capable of supporting multiple monster systems.

2009–2010 — Synchro Era Acceleration

Blackwings, Lightsworn, Gladiator Beasts, X-Sabers, Infernities, Frog strategies and other decks demonstrate the increasing importance of Special Summoning, Extra Deck access and explosive turns.

2011 — Xyz Summoning

Xyz Monsters introduce Ranks instead of Levels and use monsters as attached Xyz Materials. Generation Force begins the TCG's major Xyz era.

2012–2013 — Archetypes and Resource Engines Intensify

Inzektor, Dino Rabbit, Wind-Up, Mermail, Fire Fist, Dragon Ruler and Spellbook eras demonstrate how archetype engines and searchable card interactions can define entire competitive formats.

2014 — Pendulum Summoning

Pendulum Monsters blur the distinction between Monster and Spell Cards. Duelist Alliance also begins an enormously influential product era associated with strategies such as Shaddoll, Burning Abyss and Satellarknight.

2015–2016 — Pendulum Era and Archetype Specialization

Nekroz, Qliphort, Burning Abyss, Performapal variants, Kozmo, Monarch, Blue-Eyes and numerous other strategies show how advanced deck identity has become.

2017 — Link Summoning and Master Rule Changes

Link Monsters introduce Link Ratings, Link Arrows and Extra Monster Zones. The rules initially place major restrictions on where Extra Deck monsters can be summoned.

2018–2019 — Link Era

Sky Striker, Gouki, Trickstar, Thunder Dragon, Salamangreat, Orcust and similar strategies help define the competitive Link era. In 2019, Kouki Kosaka wins Worlds with Salamangreat.

2020 — Pandemic Disruption and Rule Revision

In-person organized play is heavily disrupted globally. Rules governing where Fusion, Synchro and Xyz Monsters can be summoned from the Extra Deck are relaxed compared with the original Link-era restriction.

2021 — Formal Rules Clarifications

Konami publishes significant rule updates clarifying Extra Deck placement, Trigger Effects, Trap Monsters and other interactions for official play.

2022 — Yu-Gi-Oh! MASTER DUEL Era

Master Duel gives the franchise a major global digital platform using modern full-format mechanics. The game brings competitive deck construction and ranked play to a large international online audience.

2023 — World Championship Returns

The World Championship returns after the 2020–2022 interruption. Paulie Aronson wins the physical-card TCG event using Dragon Link. The card game's 25th-anniversary celebration also drives major reprint and collector products.

2024 — Seattle Worlds and Anniversary Collecting

Ruben Penaranda wins the TCG World Championship in Seattle. The broader competitive event includes TCG, Master Duel and two Duel Links categories. Quarter Century products remain central to the collector side of the game.

2025 — Paris World Championship

Julien Kehon wins the TCG World Championship in Paris, defeating Tom Kleinegraeber. The year also marks the closing stages of the Quarter Century rarity cycle and introduces another generation of competitive themes.

2026 — Tokyo / Ariake World Championship

Mario Angelo De Micco of Canada defeats Josh Kelly of the United Kingdom to win the 2026 physical-card TCG World Championship. The season includes products such as Burst Protocol, Maze of Muertos, Rarity Collection V, Blazing Dominion, Battles of Legend: Glorious Gallery, Chaos Origins and Limited Pack WORLD CHAMPIONSHIP 2026.

5. Fundamental Rules — How a Yu-Gi-Oh! Duel Works

Yu-Gi-Oh! is a two-player strategy card game in its standard form. Each player brings a legal Deck and attempts to win a Duel through battle, effects, resource management and strategic sequencing. Tournament Matches commonly consist of multiple Duels, allowing Side Deck use between games.

Primary Victory Conditions

Reduce Life Points to Zero

Players normally begin a standard Duel with 8000 Life Points. Battle damage and effect damage can reduce the opponent's Life Points.

Opponent Cannot Draw

If a player is required to draw a card but has no card available to draw from the Main Deck, that player loses. Strategies built around exhausting the Deck are commonly described as Deck-out strategies.

Alternate Win Conditions

Certain cards create their own victory conditions. Exodia the Forbidden One is the best-known example: assembling all five required pieces in the hand produces an immediate win.

Standard Turn Structure

Phase Purpose
Draw Phase The turn begins. Under normal circumstances the turn player performs the required draw, subject to rules and effects affecting the first turn or draw actions.
Standby Phase Effects specifically requiring the Standby Phase activate or resolve here.
Main Phase 1 The turn player can normally summon monsters, activate appropriate card effects, Set cards, change legal battle positions and perform Extra Deck summons.
Battle Phase Eligible monsters may attack. The Battle Phase contains several internal Steps including the Start Step, Battle Step, Damage Step and End Step.
Main Phase 2 After conducting a Battle Phase, the turn player can take many of the same Main Phase actions again, subject to cards and actions already used.
End Phase The turn concludes. End Phase effects and cleanup actions occur here.
Going First vs. Going Second

The player going first has the strategic advantage of establishing monsters, Spells, Traps and disruptive effects before the opponent begins a normal turn. However, the first player does not conduct an ordinary opening Battle Phase and modern rules also alter the first turn's draw procedure.

Going-second strategies are often designed around breaking an established field. These decks may prioritize board-clearing cards, Special Summons that do not depend on controlling a field first, powerful combat effects or cards designed to trade efficiently into multiple opposing disruptions.

Chains and Effect Resolution

Chains are one of the most important concepts separating beginner play from advanced Yu-Gi-Oh!. When an effect is activated, another legal effect may respond. Each response is placed on the Chain as a new Chain Link.

Once neither player adds another effect, the Chain resolves backward. The last effect added resolves first, followed by the effect before it, until Chain Link 1 resolves.

Because effects resolve backward, the order in which players activate cards can completely change an outcome. Players must distinguish between activation, resolution, costs, targets, conditions and actions that occur without starting a Chain.

Spell Speed

Spell Speed is a rules classification controlling which effects can respond to other effects. Broadly:

  • Spell Speed 1 effects normally cannot be activated as a response to another effect.
  • Spell Speed 2 effects include many Quick Effects, Quick-Play Spells and Trap effects.
  • Spell Speed 3 is associated with Counter Traps and is the highest standard Spell Speed.

Understanding Spell Speed is necessary for determining whether a response is legal rather than merely strategically desirable.

6. Card Types, Card Anatomy, Attributes, Types, Levels, Ranks and Links

Every Yu-Gi-Oh! card belongs to a rules category. Understanding those categories is essential because cards can look similar while behaving very differently under the rules.

Monster Cards

Normal Monsters

Normal Monsters generally have no built-in effect text and instead contain flavor text. Historically, they formed a much larger portion of ordinary decks than they do in modern competitive play.

Effect Monsters

Effect Monsters contain abilities that modify the game. Their effects can involve searching, drawing, destroying, negating, summoning, sending cards to the Graveyard, manipulating battle, changing stats and countless other actions.

Ritual Monsters

Ritual Monsters are generally kept in the Main Deck and are Special Summoned using Ritual procedures, traditionally involving Ritual Spells and monsters used as Tributes or materials.

Fusion Monsters

Fusion Monsters are placed in the Extra Deck. They list or describe required Fusion Materials and are generally summoned by a Fusion Summoning effect or another procedure allowed by their text.

Synchro Monsters

Synchro Monsters are Extra Deck monsters generally summoned using a Tuner plus one or more non-Tuner monsters whose Levels satisfy the Synchro requirement.

Xyz Monsters

Xyz Monsters have Ranks rather than Levels. Monsters used for an Xyz Summon become attached beneath the Xyz Monster as Xyz Materials and can be detached for effects.

Pendulum Monsters

Pendulum Monsters function as monsters but can also be activated in Pendulum Zones as Spell Cards. They contain a Pendulum Scale and separate Pendulum Effect text.

Link Monsters

Link Monsters use Link Ratings and Link Arrows rather than DEF values. They cannot exist in Defense Position and are deeply connected to field-zone placement.

Spell Cards

Normal Spell
Activated during an appropriate Main Phase unless another rule or effect changes when it may be used.
Quick-Play Spell
Can generally be activated from the hand during the player's own turn when legal and, after being Set, can function responsively on later turns according to Quick-Play rules.
Continuous Spell
Normally remains face-up after activation and continues applying its effect while appropriate.
Equip Spell
Becomes equipped to a monster and applies an effect to or through that equipped monster.
Field Spell
Occupies the Field Zone. Modern rules allow each player to control a Field Spell simultaneously.
Ritual Spell
Traditionally used to perform Ritual Summons, although modern Ritual strategies can use many alternative procedures.

Trap Cards

Normal Trap
Usually must first be Set and cannot ordinarily be activated during the same turn it is Set.
Continuous Trap
Remains face-up and can apply ongoing effects or reusable activated effects.
Counter Trap
High-speed Trap Cards designed to respond to particular actions or activations.
Attributes

Monster Attributes include:

  • DARK
  • LIGHT
  • EARTH
  • WATER
  • FIRE
  • WIND
  • DIVINE

Attribute matters whenever a card specifically references it. Entire strategies may be built around DARK, LIGHT, FIRE, WATER or other Attribute interactions.

Monster Types

Monster Types describe classifications such as Dragon, Spellcaster, Warrior, Machine, Fiend, Fairy, Zombie, Dinosaur, Beast, Beast-Warrior, Winged Beast, Aqua, Fish, Sea Serpent, Pyro, Thunder, Rock, Plant, Insect, Reptile, Psychic, Wyrm, Cyberse, Illusion and others used throughout the game's history.

Type support is one of Yu-Gi-Oh!'s most persistent forms of deck synergy. A strategy does not have to share an archetype name if cards can instead interact through Monster Type, Attribute, Level or another characteristic.

Level, Rank and Link Rating Are Not the Same Thing

Most Main Deck monsters and several Extra Deck monster categories use Levels.

Xyz Monsters use Ranks, not Levels. Unless a card effect specifically treats them otherwise, effects that require a particular Level do not automatically treat a Rank as that Level.

Link Monsters use a Link Rating. The Link Rating helps determine the material requirements of Link Summons and corresponds to the number of Link Arrows printed on the monster.

7. Every Major Summoning Method and How the System Evolved
Normal Summon and Normal Set

A Normal Summon is the basic method of placing a monster from the hand onto the field face-up. Under ordinary rules, a player receives one standard Normal Summon/Set each turn, although card effects can provide additional opportunities.

A player may instead Set a monster face-down in Defense Position. Face-down monsters hide their identity until they are flipped face-up, attacked or otherwise revealed by game mechanics or effects.

Tribute Summon

Tribute Summoning is a form of Normal Summon used for high-Level monsters.

Under ordinary rules:

  • Level 5 and Level 6 monsters generally require one Tribute.
  • Level 7 and higher monsters generally require two Tributes.

Card effects can alter these requirements. Monarch strategies became one of Yu-Gi-Oh!'s most historically important examples of a deck designed specifically to turn Tribute Summoning into a competitive resource engine.

Flip Summon

A player can manually change an eligible face-down Defense Position monster into face-up Attack Position. This is a Flip Summon.

Older Yu-Gi-Oh! formats made especially heavy use of Flip Effects and defensive Sets. Monsters such as Magician of Faith, Fiber Jar and various Gravekeeper monsters demonstrate how important face-down monster interaction could become in slower historical formats.

Special Summon

Special Summon is a broad category rather than a single procedure. A monster can be Special Summoned by its own effect, another card's effect, or a recognized game mechanic.

Modern Yu-Gi-Oh! often revolves around sequences of multiple Special Summons in the same turn. Unless a rule, card text or restriction says otherwise, the game does not impose a global once-per-turn limit on Special Summoning.

Ritual Summoning

Ritual Summoning is one of the oldest specialized summoning systems. Traditional Ritual Summons use a Ritual Spell to summon a Ritual Monster from the hand by offering monsters whose combined Levels satisfy the Ritual requirement.

Historically, Ritual decks faced an inherent card-economy problem: the player often needed the Ritual Monster, Ritual Spell and Tribute material simultaneously. Later archetypes solved this by making Ritual pieces searchable, reusable or useful from the Graveyard.

Nekroz became one of the most famous examples of a powerful modernized Ritual strategy.

Fusion Summoning

Fusion Monsters were the original major Extra Deck monster category. Classic Fusion Summoning commonly involved Polymerization and specifically named Fusion Materials.

Early Fusion designs could be resource-intensive because a player often needed three cards simply to produce one Fusion Monster: two materials plus Polymerization.

Later design radically expanded Fusion mechanics. Cards can fuse from the field, hand, Graveyard or even Deck when their text permits it. Archetype-specific Fusion cards often provide additional benefits or recycle resources.

HERO, Cyber Dragon, Shaddoll, Invoked, Despia/Branded, Chimera and many other strategies demonstrate how Fusion evolved from a novelty-like mechanic into a major competitive system.

Synchro Summoning — Introduced to the TCG in 2008

Synchro Summoning arrived alongside the Yu-Gi-Oh! 5D's era. The basic procedure traditionally requires:

  • one Tuner monster;
  • one or more non-Tuner monsters;
  • combined Levels equal to the Level of the Synchro Monster.

Synchros changed deck building because a player no longer needed to draw a Fusion Spell and specifically named Fusion Materials. Any legal combination of appropriate monsters could become an Extra Deck threat.

Synchro design encouraged decks to manipulate Levels and produce Tuners efficiently. Stardust Dragon, Black Rose Dragon, Brionac, Trishula and numerous others became symbols of the era.

Xyz Summoning — Introduced to the TCG in 2011

Xyz Summoning usually overlays two or more monsters of the same Level. Those monsters become Xyz Materials beneath the summoned Xyz Monster.

Xyz Monsters have Ranks instead of Levels. Their effects often require the controller to detach Xyz Material, giving the monster a built-in resource system.

The mechanic dramatically expanded generic Extra Deck accessibility. Two Level 4 monsters, for example, could become any legal Rank 4 monster whose material requirements they met. Rank 4 toolboxes became especially important throughout Yu-Gi-Oh! history.

Pendulum Summoning — Introduced During the ARC-V Era

Pendulum Monsters are hybrid cards. They can function as monsters or be activated as Spells in Pendulum Zones. Each Pendulum Monster has a Pendulum Scale.

When two Pendulum Scales establish a valid range, the player can perform a Pendulum Summon of eligible monsters whose Levels fall between those scales.

Pendulum Monsters sent from the field to the Graveyard under appropriate circumstances instead go face-up to the Extra Deck, creating a recurring resource mechanic.

Pendulum rules changed significantly during the Link era. Pendulum Monsters being summoned from the face-up Extra Deck remain subject to zone-placement restrictions that do not currently apply in the same way to Fusion, Synchro and Xyz Monsters.

Link Summoning — Introduced in 2017

Link Monsters introduced one of the largest structural changes to Yu-Gi-Oh!'s field. They have Link Ratings and Link Arrows instead of DEF.

A Link Summon sends appropriate face-up monsters from the field to the Graveyard as Link Materials. Material requirements vary according to the Link Monster.

Link Arrows point toward specific adjacent zones. During the original Link-era Master Rule, these arrows played an especially restrictive role in determining where players could summon additional Extra Deck monsters.

Later rule revisions loosened these restrictions for Fusion, Synchro and Xyz monsters, but Link Monsters summoned from the Extra Deck and Pendulum Monsters summoned from the face-up Extra Deck continue to interact strongly with Extra Monster Zones and linked Main Monster Zones.

8. Major Rule-System Changes and Master Rule Evolution

Yu-Gi-Oh!'s underlying rules have not remained frozen. The game has repeatedly changed terminology, field layout and mechanical procedures to accommodate new card types.

Early Game — Fusion Deck

Before Synchro, Xyz and Link Monsters existed, the secondary monster deck was effectively associated with Fusion Monsters and was commonly called the Fusion Deck.

Synchro Era — Extra Deck Terminology

As Synchro Monsters joined Fusion Monsters, the Fusion Deck evolved into the broader Extra Deck.

Xyz Era

Xyz Monsters added Ranks and attached Materials, forcing players to distinguish Levels from Ranks and introducing a new resource beneath monster cards.

Pendulum Era

Pendulum Zones and face-up Extra Deck cards changed the field and created a hybrid Monster/Spell mechanic.

2017 Link / New Master Rule

Extra Monster Zones and Link Monsters were introduced. Extra Deck summons were initially heavily tied to Extra Monster Zones and zones pointed to by Link Monsters.

2020–2021 Revision

Fusion, Synchro and Xyz Monsters could again be initially summoned from the Extra Deck to ordinary Main Monster Zones without requiring a Link Arrow or Extra Monster Zone.

Current placement rule to remember: Fusion, Synchro and Xyz Monsters are not generally restricted to an Extra Monster Zone or Linked Zone when first summoned from the Extra Deck. Link Monsters summoned from the Extra Deck and Pendulum Monsters summoned from the face-up Extra Deck remain subject to the relevant Extra Monster Zone / Link Arrow rules.

9. Deck Construction — Main Deck, Extra Deck and Side Deck

Main Deck

A standard constructed Main Deck contains 40 to 60 cards.

Competitive decks frequently remain near 40 cards when consistency is prioritized, although many legitimate strategies intentionally play more.

Extra Deck

The Extra Deck may contain up to 15 cards.

Depending on the strategy, it can contain Fusion, Synchro, Xyz, Link and face-down Pendulum Monster Cards that begin there according to applicable rules.

Side Deck

The Side Deck contains up to 15 cards in standard tournament play. Players use it between Duels in a Match to adapt to an opponent's strategy.

The Three-Copy Rule

Under ordinary deck-construction rules, a player may use no more than three copies of the same card across the Main Deck, Extra Deck and Side Deck combined.

The Forbidden & Limited List can reduce that maximum to two, one or zero.

Engine, Starter, Extender, Payoff and Non-Engine

Modern players often describe deck components using functional terminology:

Starter
A card capable of beginning the deck's central sequence.
Extender
A card that continues a sequence, adds another body to the field or helps play through disruption.
Engine
A package of mutually supporting cards that produces the deck's central resource loop.
Payoff
A powerful result the engine is designed to access, such as a boss monster, disruption or recurring resource.
Non-Engine
Cards not required for the core engine but included to interact with opponents, break boards or address specific matchups.
10. Competitive Strategy — Card Advantage, Tempo, Interaction and Deck Roles

Competitive Yu-Gi-Oh! cannot be understood simply by asking which monster has the highest ATK. High-level play involves resource conversion, probability, sequencing, matchup knowledge, information management and understanding how effects interact.

Card Advantage

Card advantage measures the relationship between resources spent and resources gained. A card that removes two opposing cards at the cost of one can theoretically create positive advantage.

This concept is more complicated in modern Yu-Gi-Oh! because a card sent to the Graveyard may become more useful rather than less useful. A seemingly negative exchange can be desirable if it loads the Graveyard with effects or enables a larger engine.

Tempo

Tempo describes practical control over the pace and development of a Duel. A player may accept a neutral or negative card exchange if it prevents the opponent from reaching a much stronger board.

Interaction and Disruption

Modern decks attempt to interact through effects such as:

  • negating activations;
  • negating effects;
  • destroying cards;
  • banishing cards;
  • returning cards to the hand or Deck;
  • changing control;
  • preventing Special Summons;
  • restricting card types;
  • removing Graveyard resources;
  • interrupting searches or draws.
Combo Decks

Combo decks use sequences of mutually enabling effects to convert opening cards into an established field or decisive game state. Their strength comes from synergy and conversion efficiency rather than any single card viewed in isolation.

Combo decks must consider interruption points, alternate lines, resource requirements and how to continue if the opponent stops an early effect.

Control Decks

Control strategies attempt to restrict the opponent's options, repeatedly trade resources and win through long-term advantage. Trap-heavy decks are the classic example, although modern control can also be driven by monsters, Graveyard effects or recurring Spells.

Midrange Decks

Midrange strategies attempt to combine efficient development with resilient interaction. They may not create the most explosive possible opening board, but their engine often continues producing value after the first exchange.

OTK and FTK

OTK means One-Turn Kill: a player reduces the opponent from a stable amount of Life Points to zero during one turn.

FTK means First-Turn Kill: a strategy wins before the opponent receives a normal opportunity to take their first turn.

FTKs have repeatedly influenced Forbidden & Limited List decisions because highly consistent non-interactive wins can undermine ordinary back-and-forth gameplay.

Hand Traps

"Hand trap" is community terminology for cards that can interact from the hand during an opponent's turn. They are not necessarily Trap Cards.

Cards such as Effect Veiler, Maxx "C" in environments where legal, Ash Blossom & Joyous Spring, Droll & Lock Bird and Nibiru, the Primal Being became major landmarks in how modern players defend themselves before establishing a field.

11. Tournament Formats, Forbidden Cards and the Forbidden & Limited List

Yu-Gi-Oh! does not rely on a single permanent card pool without restrictions. Konami periodically changes card legality through Forbidden & Limited Lists.

Forbidden

A Forbidden card cannot be included in the Main Deck, Extra Deck or Side Deck for the applicable format.

Limited

Only one copy may be included across the player's Main, Extra and Side Deck combined.

Semi-Limited

Up to two copies may be used across the player's Decks.

The exact list changes over time. A historical deck list must therefore be understood in the context of the restriction list that existed when the event occurred.

Current archival note: As of this page's September 22, 2026 research update, Konami's official TCG site identifies a Forbidden & Limited List effective May 18, 2026. Always check the official list before entering an event because later revisions can supersede it.

Current Official Forbidden & Limited List

Advanced Format

Advanced Format is the principal competitive constructed environment and follows the current Forbidden & Limited List.

Traditional Format

Traditional Format historically allowed cards designated Forbidden in Advanced Format to be played in restricted quantities under Traditional rules. It is distinct from ordinary modern Advanced tournament play.

Time Wizard / Historical Formats

Time Wizard-style play recreates earlier periods of the game using the card pool, rules and Forbidden & Limited List associated with a specified historical date.

This approach allows players to experience formats such as Goat or Edison without applying the modern card pool.

Speed Duel

Speed Duel uses a streamlined rule set inspired by Duel Links. It uses smaller Decks and Skill Cards and is designed to produce a different experience from the standard full TCG.

12. Yu-Gi-Oh! Product Types — Boosters, Decks, Tins, Tournament Packs and Collector Products

Yu-Gi-Oh! cards have been distributed through many product types. Understanding those categories is important when reconstructing where a card originally came from.

Core / Main Booster Sets

Large booster sets that introduce substantial quantities of new cards and often define major stages of the competitive card pool.

Side Sets

Supplemental booster products can introduce new archetypes, reprints, collector treatments or anime-focused cards outside the primary core-set schedule.

Starter Decks

Preconstructed entry products designed to give players a playable starting Deck and basic instruction.

Structure Decks

Themed preconstructed Decks built around a strategy, archetype, character or mechanic. Some Structure Decks become competitively significant because multiple copies can provide the core of a tournament strategy.

Tins

Collectible tins commonly combine packs, promotional cards and reprints. Tin design has changed significantly across Yu-Gi-Oh!'s history.

Legendary Collections

Collector-focused products combining classic booster packs, reprints, promotional cards, storage items or game boards.

Duelist Packs

Products historically associated with anime characters, signature themes and strategy support.

Battles of Legend

A long-running supplemental series associated with foil cards, reprints, previously unavailable cards and special collector treatments.

Rarity Collections

Premium reprint products emphasizing popular competitive cards and multiple high-end rarity treatments.

Tournament Packs / OTS Packs

Products distributed through organized play rather than normal retail channels. They can contain reprints and special rarity cards used to support local tournament participation.

Promotional Cards

Cards distributed through magazines, video games, theatrical releases, events, books, championships, promotional campaigns and other limited channels.

World Championship Products

World Championship prize cards, commemorative products and limited packs connect Yu-Gi-Oh!'s competitive history directly to the physical collector market.

14. Special Editions, Promotional Cards, Tins, Legendary Collections and Tournament Products

Booster packs represent only one part of Yu-Gi-Oh!'s physical history. Many historically important cards entered circulation through promotional products or special editions.

Special Edition Products

Traditional Special Edition releases often bundled multiple booster packs with promotional cards. These products helped redistribute cards and sometimes introduced previews of cards associated with later sets.

Collector Tins

Tins became a major annual product format. Early tins often highlighted famous monsters as promotional cards, while later tins evolved into large reprint products containing specially constructed Mega-Packs and promos.

Video-Game Promotional Cards

Physical cards bundled with Yu-Gi-Oh! video games became an important collecting tradition. Some cards were highly desired because purchasing the game was originally the primary route for obtaining them.

Game Boy, Game Boy Advance, Nintendo DS and other Yu-Gi-Oh! games therefore occupy an unusual place in card-game history: the software and physical card market directly supported one another.

Magazine and Book Promotions

Shonen Jump, V Jump and other publications became major distribution channels for promotional Yu-Gi-Oh! cards. Promotional distribution sometimes gave collectors access to cards long before a conventional booster reprint appeared.

Tournament Pack Lineage

Yu-Gi-Oh! has used multiple generations of tournament-support packs. These included early Tournament Packs and later Official Tournament Store products.

The modern OTS / Ultimate Tournament Pack system rewards participation at approved stores and provides a route for special rarity reprints.

Konami lists Ultimate Tournament Pack 1 as a July 2026 product and Ultimate Tournament Pack 2 for November 2026, continuing the long tradition of non-standard retail tournament packs.

Legendary Collection

The original Legendary Collection brought together six famous early booster packs:

  • Legend of Blue-Eyes White Dragon
  • Metal Raiders
  • Spell Ruler
  • Pharaoh's Servant
  • Dark Crisis
  • Invasion of Chaos

It also emphasized iconic monsters and Egyptian God Card promotional material, helping establish the concept of a nostalgia-driven collector product.

Legendary Collection: 25th Anniversary Edition

In 2023, the Legendary Collection concept returned as part of the 25th Anniversary celebration. The product contained the six classic packs associated with the earlier collection and introduced collectors to Quarter Century Secret Rare treatments through a bonus card.

Lost Art Promotion

Lost Art promotional cards became notable because they could feature artwork closer to versions used outside earlier localized TCG printings.

They are historically relevant to the study of localization, censorship, artwork alteration and collector preference.

15. Complete Rarity History — Common Through Modern Collector Rarities

Yu-Gi-Oh! uses rarity not only to control how often cards appear, but also as an artistic and collector system. The same card can exist in multiple rarities across different releases.

Common

The standard non-premium treatment. Commons generally lack the major foil treatments associated with higher rarities.

Rare

Traditionally associated with foil lettering on the card name while the artwork itself remains non-holographic.

Super Rare

Historically recognized by holographic artwork without the same gold-name combination used for classic Ultra Rares.

Ultra Rare

A classic premium rarity typically combining foil artwork and metallic card-name lettering.

Secret Rare

Traditionally features a distinctive diagonal holographic pattern and bright foil lettering.

Ultimate Rare

A premium embossed treatment associated historically with booster sets and later tournament products.

Ghost Rare

Known for a highly reflective, ghost-like image treatment that changes substantially depending on viewing angle and light.

Gold Rare

Used in Gold Series and related products with distinctive gold detailing.

Platinum Rare

A premium metallic treatment used in selected collector and reprint products.

Collector's Rare

A textured premium rarity used in selected side sets and collector-focused products.

Starlight Rare

An extremely premium modern rarity characterized by extensive foil treatment. Starlight Rare continued evolving visually during the 2026 product era.

Quarter Century Secret Rare

Created for the 25th Anniversary celebration and used across major anniversary products before the cycle concluded.

Why the Same Card Can Have Many Prices

A Yu-Gi-Oh! card's collector value can vary based on:

  • set;
  • rarity;
  • edition;
  • language;
  • region;
  • print variation;
  • artwork;
  • condition;
  • professional grading;
  • competitive demand;
  • historical importance;
  • distribution quantity.

Therefore, a famous card name alone does not identify a collectible version. Set code and printing details matter.

First Edition and Unlimited Edition

Historically, many TCG products distinguished initial First Edition printings from later Unlimited Edition printings. For collectors, this distinction can greatly affect desirability.

Not every Yu-Gi-Oh! product follows the same printing structure, so edition markings should always be interpreted in the context of the specific set.

16. Organized Play — Locals, Regionals, YCS, WCQ and the Road to Worlds

Competitive Yu-Gi-Oh! developed into an international tournament system with multiple levels of organized play.

Official Tournament Stores

OTS locations form the local foundation of organized play. Local tournaments give players regular opportunities to test decks and compete.

Regional Qualifiers

Larger tournaments can award invitations or qualification toward higher-level championship competition.

Yu-Gi-Oh! Championship Series

YCS events are major open tournaments drawing large fields and often serving as important snapshots of the contemporary metagame.

World Championship Qualifiers

WCQ systems determine which players earn the right to represent their regions at the World Championship.

World Championship

The World Championship brings qualified competitors from multiple regions together under event-specific card legality and tournament rules.

Why World Championship Decks Can Differ From Ordinary TCG Decks

OCG and TCG card pools and restriction lists differ. A normal regional tournament can therefore permit cards that are unavailable or restricted differently elsewhere.

Worlds needs one common environment. Event-specific legality rules can produce a metagame unlike either ordinary TCG or ordinary OCG competition.

18. Famous Historical Formats and Competitive Eras
Early Beatdown

In the earliest TCG environment, efficient Normal Summons and high ATK mattered enormously. Monsters such as La Jinn the Mystical Genie and Summoned Skull became benchmarks because many decks lacked the specialized engine structure associated with later Yu-Gi-Oh!.

Hand Control and Yata-Lock

Early Yu-Gi-Oh! contained exceptionally powerful hand-control cards. The infamous Yata-Garasu lock could create a situation in which a player was repeatedly prevented from rebuilding resources through the normal Draw Phase.

The strength of these interactions became part of the history leading toward more aggressive Forbidden & Limited List management.

Chaos Era

LIGHT and DARK monsters became an extraordinarily powerful resource combination when Chaos monsters rewarded players for banishing those Attributes from the Graveyard.

Black Luster Soldier - Envoy of the Beginning and Chaos Emperor Dragon - Envoy of the End are among the best-known historical examples of cards whose raw power shaped an era.

Goat Format

Goat Format is a community name for a widely replayed 2005 historical format. The name references Scapegoat, although the format contains many strategies beyond literal Goat Control.

The format is valued for slow resource management, hand reading, battle-position decisions, bluffing and the impact of individually powerful limited cards.

TeleDAD

Teleport Dark Armed Dragon combined Emergency Teleport, Tuners, Destiny HERO resources and Dark Armed Dragon into one of the most famous strategies of early Synchro-era competitive Yu-Gi-Oh!.

Edison Format

Edison Format recreates the card pool and restrictions around the 2010 Shonen Jump Championship in Edison, New Jersey. It has developed into one of the most popular historical Yu-Gi-Oh! formats.

The environment supports numerous strategies and represents an era in which Synchro Summoning was well established but later mechanics such as Xyz did not yet exist.

Dragon Ruler / Spellbook Era

2013 is remembered for extraordinarily powerful Dragon Ruler and Spellbook strategies. Dragon Rulers could repeatedly convert hand and Graveyard resources into high-Level monsters and Extra Deck access.

Spellbook benefited from an extremely powerful search and resource engine. The format is frequently referenced when players debate the strongest decks in Yu-Gi-Oh! history.

Duelist Alliance Era

The 2014 Duelist Alliance era introduced a generation of highly synergistic strategies, including Shaddoll, Satellarknight and Burning Abyss.

Their ability to replace resources and activate effects from places beyond the hand made them feel structurally modern compared with many earlier decks.

Pendulum Combination Decks

Performapal, Performage and Pendulum Magician engines demonstrated how explosive Pendulum Summoning could become when scales, searches and Extra Deck recursion aligned.

TOSS Format

"TOSS" is a community shorthand often associated with the late-2018/2019 environment dominated for long periods by Thunder Dragon, Orcust, Sky Striker and Salamangreat.

The term captures a particularly memorable period of Link-era competitive Yu-Gi-Oh! where several strong strategies remained relevant across multiple events.

Modern Engine-Based Yu-Gi-Oh!

Modern decks frequently combine compact archetypal packages. One engine can provide starters or interruptions while leaving room for another engine and substantial non-engine interaction.

This makes deck building increasingly modular. Competitive innovation often involves discovering how separate engines overlap through Monster Type, Attribute, Level, Extra Deck access or shared resource requirements.

19. Yu-Gi-Oh! Video Games, Digital Card Games and Their Relationship to the TCG

Yu-Gi-Oh! has one of the longest video-game histories of any major trading-card franchise. Digital games served several different purposes: recreating the physical game, promoting cards, telling anime-inspired stories and offering alternate rule systems.

Early Game Boy and Game Boy Advance Games

Early handheld Yu-Gi-Oh! games introduced many players to deck construction before they understood the full tournament game. Some titles used rule sets or card interactions that differed from the contemporary physical game.

Promotional cards bundled with games also made the video-game line directly relevant to physical collectors.

World Championship Video-Game Series

Nintendo handheld World Championship titles attempted to recreate increasingly complete versions of the physical card game while adding AI opponents, puzzle challenges, campaign progression and tournament structures.

Before modern online simulators and Master Duel, these games were among the most accessible ways to practice full Yu-Gi-Oh! rules without another human player physically present.

Yu-Gi-Oh! Duel Links

Duel Links introduced a faster digital format based around a reduced field, smaller Decks and character Skills.

It eventually became important enough to receive its own World Championship competition. Rush Duel was later added to the application as a separate experience.

Yu-Gi-Oh! MASTER DUEL

MASTER DUEL launched as a global digital platform intended to present modern full-format Yu-Gi-Oh! with large-scale online matchmaking, solo content, deck construction and competitive events.

Its exact card pool and Forbidden & Limited List are not always identical to the physical TCG, so a legal Master Duel deck should not automatically be assumed legal in a current paper TCG event.

Master Duel later became a World Championship category, with team-based competition used at the highest level.

20. Collecting, Card Identification and Historical Preservation

Yu-Gi-Oh! cards are both game pieces and collectible historical objects. Correctly identifying a card requires more information than simply reading its name.

Information to Record When Archiving a Card

  • Card name.
  • Set code.
  • Collector / card number.
  • Rarity.
  • Edition marking.
  • Language.
  • Region.
  • Copyright / template information.
  • Artwork variation.
  • Condition.
  • Whether the card is tournament legal or a non-playable promotional reproduction.
Set Codes

Set codes allow two visually similar cards to be distinguished. A Blue-Eyes White Dragon from one product is not necessarily the same collector printing as a Blue-Eyes White Dragon from another.

When cataloging a collection, set code should be preserved together with rarity and edition.

Condition

Collectors evaluate wear including scratching, edge damage, whitening, bends, creases, dents and surface damage.

Competitive players may accept visibly played cards for casual use, but tournament cards must still meet event requirements and cannot be distinguishable in a way that creates marked-card concerns.

Card Sleeves

Sleeves protect cards from normal handling and are essential for valuable cards used in play. Tournament policies can set requirements for consistency and condition because marked sleeves can reveal card identity.

Reprints and Historical Accessibility

Reprints are essential to Yu-Gi-Oh!'s ecosystem. A card that begins as a difficult-to-find high-rarity card may later appear in a Structure Deck, tin, Rarity Collection, tournament pack or other product.

This means the history of a card includes not only its first appearance but also the history of when it became affordable or broadly accessible to players.

22. Yu-Gi-Oh! Terminology and Common Competitive Vocabulary
Archetype
A group of cards connected through a shared name or card text. Examples include HERO, Blue-Eyes, Dark Magician, Blackwing, Sky Striker and countless others.
Engine
A package of cards that work together to generate a repeatable resource or sequence.
Brick
Community slang for a card that is difficult or undesirable to draw in a particular situation.
Garnet
Community slang derived from Gem-Knight Garnet for a card that is needed in the Deck for an engine but is significantly worse when drawn naturally.
Floodgate
Community terminology for an effect that broadly restricts actions, card types or game mechanics rather than trading for only one isolated opposing card.
Board Breaker
A card designed to dismantle an established field, often when going second.
Hand Trap
Community terminology for interactive cards usable from the hand during an opponent's turn.
Boss Monster
A major payoff monster that represents a deck's strongest or most recognizable field presence.
Search
To add a specified card from the Deck to the hand through an effect.
Mill
Community terminology for sending cards from the Deck to the Graveyard.
Bounce
Community terminology for returning a card from the field to the hand.
Spin
Community terminology often used when a card is returned or shuffled from the field into the Deck.
Topdeck

25. Historical Summary — How Yu-Gi-Oh! Changed Over More Than Two Decades

Yu-Gi-Oh! began as an imagined card battle inside a manga about many kinds of games. Its popularity transformed that fictional concept into a physical game whose real competitive history eventually became far more complicated than the fictional rules that inspired it.

The earliest physical game emphasized individual monsters, battle statistics, resource conservation and powerful standalone Spells and Traps. As the card pool expanded, combinations became more important. Chaos decks taught players how strongly the Graveyard could function as a resource. Monarchs showed how Tribute Summoning could support an entire strategy. Gladiator Beasts rewarded combat and Deck interaction. Synchro Summoning changed the Extra Deck from a Fusion-only concept into a reusable toolbox.

Xyz Summoning made generic Extra Deck access even easier. Pendulum Summoning challenged the traditional boundary between Monster and Spell Cards. Link Summoning redesigned the physical field itself and briefly made Link infrastructure essential for almost every Extra Deck strategy.

At the same time, archetypes became more coherent. Search effects, Graveyard effects and recursive engines made modern decks far more consistent. A card was no longer evaluated only by what it did immediately after being drawn. Players began evaluating how it interacted with a network of cards in the Deck, Extra Deck, Graveyard and banished area.

Competitive Yu-Gi-Oh! similarly evolved from regional tournaments and early World Championships into a sophisticated international system involving local Official Tournament Stores, Regional Qualifiers, Championship Series events, World Championship Qualifiers and global finals.

Digital Yu-Gi-Oh! expanded alongside the physical game. Handheld World Championship games let players practice against AI. Duel Links created an alternate Speed Duel ecosystem. Master Duel created a modern global digital platform and eventually became part of the World Championship structure itself.

The collector side of the game also became increasingly complex. A single monster can now exist in Commons, Rares, Super Rares, Ultra Rares, Secret Rares, Ultimate Rares, Ghost Rares, Collector's Rares, Starlight Rares, anniversary rarities, alternate arts and promotional printings.

More than two decades after the Konami OCG began, Yu-Gi-Oh! therefore exists simultaneously as a competitive game, a collector hobby, an anime and manga franchise, a digital game ecosystem and an enormous historical archive. Every new set adds another layer to a card pool that still contains recognizable monsters and mechanics originating near the very beginning of the franchise.

That continuity is one of the most distinctive parts of Yu-Gi-Oh!'s history: a player in 2026 can encounter Blue-Eyes White Dragon, Dark Magician, Exodia or Red-Eyes cards and still trace those monsters back to the fictional duels that helped create the real card game in the first place.
