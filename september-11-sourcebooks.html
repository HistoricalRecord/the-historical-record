How to Read the Historical Record

A document archive is most useful when readers understand what different types of sources actually represent.

Primary Sources

Original government records, directives, transcripts, memoranda, reports, correspondence, testimony, and other records created during the period being studied.

Government Reports

Official investigations and reports that synthesize evidence and present findings. These should be distinguished from the underlying documents on which they rely.

Archival Collections

Curated collections assembled by institutions such as the National Security Archive. An archival collection can bring together records from multiple original government repositories.

Later Releases

Documents that became publicly available after the original events through declassification, litigation, archival work, Freedom of Information processes, or other release mechanisms.

The 9/11 Commission Report

Begin with the official account before examining the underlying source material.

FINAL REPORT • JULY 22, 2004
Government Report Official Record

Final Report of the National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States

The National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States was established to prepare a full and complete account of the circumstances surrounding the September 11 attacks, including preparedness for and the immediate response to the attacks.

The report is an essential starting point because it provides a broad narrative and organizes findings across terrorism, intelligence, aviation security, emergency response, national policy, and other areas.

Research Timeline

A broad chronological framework for placing the documents in historical context.

1970s–1980s

Afghanistan and the Cold War

The historical background includes Afghanistan's political development, the Soviet intervention, the war, and the international forces that shaped the country.

1988–1992

Formation and Early Development of al-Qaeda

The Commission's account examines the emergence of bin Laden and al-Qaeda and the organization's development.

1993

First World Trade Center Bombing

The bombing became an important part of the evolving U.S. counterterrorism picture examined by later investigations.

1996–1998

Bin Laden in Afghanistan

The Commission describes al-Qaeda's renewal in Afghanistan and the increasing international terrorism threat.

1998

Embassy Bombings

The U.S. embassy bombings in Kenya and Tanzania became major events in the evolution of U.S. counterterrorism policy.

2000

USS Cole Attack

The attack against the USS Cole became an important case study in the pre-9/11 terrorism record.

Summer 2001

The Summer of Threat

The Commission examined intelligence warnings and late leads concerning the developing threat.

September 11, 2001

The Attacks

Four hijacked commercial aircraft were used in the attacks on the World Trade Center, the Pentagon, and the attempted attack represented by the crash in Pennsylvania.

2001–2002

Investigation and Institutional Response

Government agencies, Congress, investigators, intelligence organizations, and other institutions began extensive investigations and policy responses.

2002

Creation of the 9/11 Commission

Congress and the President established the National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States.

2004

Final Commission Report

The Commission released its final report on July 22, 2004.

Later Years

Continued Declassification and Archival Releases

Additional records have continued to become available through archival collections, declassification, and later document releases.

Suggested Research Path

A manageable way to move from the broad historical account toward individual primary documents.

1
Establish the official chronology.
Begin with the Commission Report and its chapter structure. This provides the broadest framework for the material.
2
Study the development of al-Qaeda.
Use the Commission's chapters on the development of bin Laden and al-Qaeda alongside Volume I.
3
Study Afghanistan.
Use Volumes II and IV to establish historical context for Afghanistan and the Soviet war.
4
Examine the hunt for bin Laden.
Volume VI provides a focused archival collection for studying U.S. strategy and operations.
5
Study the Taliban relationship.
Use Volume VII and the later Taliban File releases to follow the diplomatic and political record.
6
Examine the aviation and emergency response record.
Review the Commission staff material and later FAA-related archival releases.
7
Return to the original documents.
When an important claim appears in a report or secondary collection, follow the archival link back to the underlying document whenever possible.

Research Methodology

Principles for using this page responsibly as a historical research tool.

1. Follow the source.

A summary on this page is not the same thing as the underlying historical document. Readers should follow links to the original archival collection whenever possible.

2. Distinguish evidence from interpretation.

A memorandum, transcript, report, testimony, intelligence assessment, and later historical interpretation are different kinds of evidence and should not automatically be treated as interchangeable.

3. Preserve context.

Individual documents can be misleading when removed from the circumstances in which they were created. Consider the date, author, intended audience, classification status, purpose, and surrounding records.

4. Compare records.

Where possible, compare government reports with the underlying records and with independent archival collections.

5. Do not confuse an archive with an endorsement.

The inclusion of a document in this research guide means that the document is historically relevant to the collection. It does not mean that The Historical Record endorses every claim contained within it.

6. Preserve the original source.

The Historical Record organizes and presents links to third-party material. Ownership and archival responsibility remain with the respective institutions, agencies, authors, publishers, and archives.
