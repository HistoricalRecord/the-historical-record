By Jonathon Bender

Heinous: Why I Cannot Stand Donald Trump

I have spent an unreasonable amount of time trying to find the right word for Donald Trump.

Awful doesn't do it. Dishonest doesn't do it. Corrupt doesn't quite do it. Cruel doesn't do it. Vulgar doesn't do it. Even sleazy—a word that seems almost custom-built for Donald Trump—feels inadequate.

The word I keep coming back to is heinous.

I don't use that word lightly. I use it because I look at the accumulated record: the criminal conviction, the sexual-abuse verdict involving E. Jean Carroll, the staggering catalogue of false and misleading statements, the documented relationship with Jeffrey Epstein, the attacks on investigators and institutions, the willingness to treat government power as an extension of personal grievance, the cruelty inflicted on people with little political power, and the extraordinary willingness to gamble with the lives and economic security of millions of Americans.

This is my opinion. I am not pretending this section is objective journalism. It isn't.

But my opinion did not materialize out of thin air. It came from watching the record accumulate.

Donald Trump was found guilty by a New York jury on 34 felony counts of falsifying business records. A federal jury also found him liable for sexually abusing E. Jean Carroll and defaming her. Those facts belong in the historical record regardless of whether someone approves of Trump politically.

Then there are the lies. Not one lie. Not ten. A seemingly endless stream of false and misleading statements repeated with such frequency that dishonesty has become, in my view, one of the operating mechanisms of his political career.

There is the 9/11 mythology, including his repeatedly disputed claims about seeing thousands of Muslims celebrating the attacks. There is his characterization of the Mueller investigation as a hoax and a witch hunt, despite Mueller's report not providing the unconditional exoneration Trump repeatedly claimed.

There is Jeffrey Epstein and the documented history of their association. I will not pretend that association alone proves participation in Epstein's crimes. It doesn't. But I believe the relationship and the questions surrounding it deserve scrutiny.

There is the Reflecting Pool episode, where accusations of vandalism were followed by evidence pointing toward problems with the renovation itself. To me, it represents something larger: the danger of establishing a villain before establishing the facts.

There are also the questions surrounding financial markets and Trump's ability to move markets through presidential statements and policy announcements. I am not going to turn suspicious trading into an accusation of criminal market manipulation without evidence. But I believe those questions deserve serious investigation.

And then there is war.

War is not a television show. It is not a campaign slogan. It is not a market-moving announcement. People die. Families are shattered. Children lose parents. Parents bury children.

Finally, there is healthcare—the people who cannot afford to lose it, the families living paycheck to paycheck, the sick, the elderly, the disabled, and the millions whose access to healthcare can be determined by decisions made in Washington.

I believe government decisions that make healthcare harder to obtain for millions of people deserve to be judged not merely as budgetary calculations, but as moral decisions with human consequences.

I have seen claims about false-flag shootings, staged events, and other conspiracies surrounding Trump. I am deliberately not presenting those things as established facts. The evidence has to matter, even when the person being criticized is someone I despise.

And that is ultimately where I stand.

I don't need to invent anything about Donald Trump.

I don't need to manufacture a crime. I don't need to turn suspicion into certainty. I don't need to exaggerate a photograph or repeat an internet conspiracy.

The documented record is already extraordinary enough.

I can be furious and still be precise. I can be vulgar and still be factual. I can be merciless in my opinion without becoming careless with the truth.

Because I don't believe Donald Trump is merely an asshole.

An asshole is somebody who cuts you off in traffic. An asshole is somebody who treats a waiter badly. An asshole is somebody who ruins Thanksgiving.

Those words are too small.

Heinous is the word I choose.

Not because an insult is an argument. It isn't. The argument is the record.

The insult is my reaction to it.

And after watching that record accumulate for years, I no longer feel any obligation to disguise my disgust in polite language simply because the person I am criticizing happens to occupy the most powerful office in the United States.

I can be furious. I can be merciless. But I will also be precise.
