OPINION

Heinous

Why I Cannot Stand Donald Trump

OPINION

SEPTEMBER 13, 2026

By Jonathon Bender

Heinous

I have spent an unreasonable amount of time trying to find the right word for Donald Trump.

Awful doesn’t do it.

Dishonest doesn’t do it.

Corrupt doesn’t quite do it.

Cruel doesn’t do it.

Vulgar doesn’t do it.

Even sleazy—a word that seems almost custom-built for Donald Trump—feels inadequate.

The word I keep coming back to is heinous.

I don’t use that word lightly.

I use it because I look at the accumulated record—the criminal conviction, the sexual-abuse verdict, the staggering catalogue of falsehoods, the documented relationship with Jeffrey Epstein, the attacks on investigators and institutions, the willingness to treat government power as an extension of personal grievance, the cruelty inflicted on people who have little political power, and the extraordinary willingness to gamble with the lives and economic security of millions of Americans—and I no longer know what softer word adequately describes my reaction.

And yes, this is an opinion.

It is my opinion.

I am not pretending this section is objective journalism. It isn’t.

But my opinion did not materialize out of thin air.

It came from watching the record accumulate.

The conviction

Donald Trump was found guilty by a New York jury on 34 felony counts of falsifying business records. The Manhattan District Attorney’s office announced the verdict on May 30, 2024. (Manhattan District Attorney’s Office)

Thirty-four.

Not thirty-four tweets.

Not thirty-four rude comments.

Thirty-four felony convictions.

I find it astonishing that this fact can be discussed as though it were merely another item in the endless carnival of American political controversy.

It isn’t.

A jury found him guilty.

And somehow we have reached a point where the response from much of the political world is not How did we get here? but How can we make this disappear from the conversation?

I refuse to make it disappear.

E. Jean Carroll

Then there is E. Jean Carroll.

A federal jury found Donald Trump liable for sexually abusing Carroll and defaming her. That judgment survived the appeals process, and in August 2026 the Supreme Court declined to hear Trump’s challenge. (TIME)

That distinction matters.

I am not saying, “I believe a woman made an accusation.”

I am saying that a jury heard the evidence and reached a verdict, the verdict was upheld, and Trump’s attempt to take the matter to the Supreme Court was rejected.

That is part of the record.

And yet the man found liable for sexually abusing a woman is once again sitting in the Oval Office.

I find that morally grotesque.

What bothers me almost as much as the conduct itself is the normalization of it.

We have become so accustomed to Trump’s scandals that something which would have ended the political career of almost anyone else becomes another paragraph in tomorrow’s news cycle.

I cannot accept that normalization.

The lies

And then there are the lies.

Not one lie.

Not ten.

Not an occasional exaggeration from a politician trying to make himself look good.

The Washington Post spent Trump’s first presidency cataloguing false and misleading claims and ultimately counted 30,573 over four years. (The Washington Post)

And the pattern did not magically disappear.

In 2025, the Washington Post examined Trump’s first 100 days of his second term and identified 32 false or misleading claims in one Time magazine interview. (The Washington Post)

AP continues to document false and misleading claims coming from Trump and his administration, including claims about jobs, foreign investment, the stock market, healthcare and the 2020 election. (AP News)

At some point, I stop thinking of dishonesty as an unfortunate characteristic of the politician.

It becomes part of the governing method.

Say it.

Repeat it.

Flood the public with it.

Make everyone argue about it.

And by the time the correction arrives, the lie has already done its work.

That is not merely irritating.

It is corrosive.

September 11

I will never forget Trump’s repeated claim that he watched thousands of Muslims in Jersey City celebrate as the World Trade Center fell.

The claim was widely debunked. FactCheck.org described it as a widely discredited assertion, and the Washington Post documented the absence of evidence supporting it. (FactCheck.org)

Twenty-five years after September 11, the falsehoods surrounding Trump’s account of that day are still being examined. They include claims about the height of his building, his participation in the cleanup, and other supposed experiences surrounding the attacks for which supporting evidence has not emerged. (The Guardian)

There is something particularly ugly about turning one of the worst days in American history into a vehicle for personal mythology.

9/11 was not Trump’s story.

It belonged to the people who died.

It belonged to the firefighters, police officers, emergency workers and civilians who ran toward the disaster.

It belonged to the families who never got their loved ones back.

And it certainly did not belong to some imaginary crowd of thousands of Muslims celebrating in New Jersey.

I find the exploitation of that tragedy contemptible.

Mueller

Then there was Robert Mueller.

Trump spent years calling the investigation a “witch hunt” and insisting that it had completely exonerated him.

It did not.

Mueller explicitly rejected the claim that his investigation had exonerated Trump. His report also documented Russian interference in the 2016 election and examined multiple episodes involving possible obstruction of justice. (PBS)

The investigation did not establish sufficient evidence to charge the Trump campaign with a criminal conspiracy with Russia.

That matters too.

I am not going to turn Mueller’s report into something it wasn’t simply because I dislike Trump.

But neither will I allow Trump to rewrite it into an unconditional exoneration that Mueller himself rejected.

That is the distinction I want this website to maintain:

I will be vicious in my opinion. I will not be careless with my facts.

Epstein

And then there is Jeffrey Epstein.

Trump’s relationship with Epstein is documented. Their social association is not a conspiracy theory. Trump’s name appears in Epstein-related records, and subsequent document releases have provided additional information about their past association. (PBS)

There are also reports concerning Trump’s presence on Epstein’s aircraft. (Al Jazeera)

But here I draw a line.

I will not tell readers that Trump’s association with Epstein proves that Trump participated in Epstein’s crimes.

It doesn’t.

Association is not proof of criminal conduct.

I don’t need to make that leap.

The documented relationship is disturbing enough to warrant questions about what Trump knew, what he knew about the people around Epstein, what their relationship involved and what information the public has a right to see.

Those questions deserve answers.

But I will not turn suspicion into fact simply because I hate the man.

That would make me no better than the people who turn Trump’s own fabrications into political ammunition.

The Reflecting Pool

Then came the Reflecting Pool.

Trump’s administration initially blamed vandalism for damage to the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool and a man was prosecuted over alleged vandalism.

The story subsequently unraveled.

The contractor responsible for the renovation acknowledged that faulty repair work caused the damage. Prosecutors dropped the case against the man who had been accused, and reporting showed that problems with the renovation itself were responsible for the peeling and bubbling that had prompted the controversy. (Reuters)

This episode infuriates me because it is such a perfect little miniature of the larger problem.

Something goes wrong.

Find someone to blame.

Point the finger.

Create outrage.

And only afterward worry about whether the evidence supports the story.

That isn’t leadership.

That’s scapegoating.

And when the machinery of government is capable of turning an unsupported presidential accusation into a criminal prosecution, I find that profoundly frightening.

Markets and the war

Then there is the money.

Trump has repeatedly demonstrated an extraordinary ability to move financial markets simply by speaking or changing policy.

But in 2026, the questions became considerably more serious.

Reuters reported that investigators were examining suspiciously timed oil trades occurring shortly before major Trump policy announcements concerning Iran. One example involved approximately $950 million in oil positions placed hours before Trump’s April announcement of a two-week ceasefire, when oil prices subsequently fell sharply. Another involved approximately $500 million in oil futures traded shortly before Trump announced a delay in attacks on Iranian energy infrastructure. (Investing.com)

Let me be extremely clear:

Suspicious trading is not proof that Donald Trump personally manipulated markets or that he personally profited from those trades.

I will not make that accusation without evidence.

But I think Americans are perfectly justified in asking why trades capable of making enormous sums of money repeatedly appeared around major presidential policy announcements.

That question deserves investigation.

And it becomes even more disturbing when the president’s decisions concern war.

Trump promised to be a president who would end wars.

Instead, the United States became involved in a prolonged conflict with Iran beginning in February 2026. Reuters reported this month that the war had become a drawn-out campaign with no clear exit strategy, rising fuel prices and growing domestic opposition. (Reuters)

And as of today, Trump is talking openly about the possibility that the United States could remain in Iran and potentially control its oil resources. He has also suggested that the war could continue beyond the November midterm elections. (Reuters)

I find that profoundly disturbing.

War is not a television show.

It is not a campaign slogan.

It is not a market-moving announcement.

People die.

Families are shattered.

Children lose parents.

Parents bury children.

And the people making those decisions do not get to treat human beings as pieces on a geopolitical chessboard.

Healthcare

And then there is the quieter cruelty.

The people who cannot afford to lose.

The people who don’t have lobbyists.

The people who don’t have millions of dollars in the bank.

The people who get sick.

The people who need Medicaid.

The people who work two jobs and still cannot afford insurance.

The 2025 reconciliation law dramatically changed Medicaid. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that the changes will reduce Medicaid enrollment by 12.9 million people by 2034 and increase the number of uninsured Americans by 7.5 million in that year. (Congressional Budget Office)

Those aren’t abstract numbers to me.

Behind every number is a person.

A diabetic.

A cancer patient.

A child.

An elderly person.

A disabled person.

A family deciding whether the electric bill gets paid before the prescription gets filled.

And when government policy knowingly makes healthcare harder to obtain for millions of people in the name of reducing federal spending, I am allowed to make a moral judgment about that.

I think it is cruel.

I think it is indefensible.

And I think pretending that spreadsheets somehow erase the human consequences is an insult to the people who will actually live with those consequences.

The false flags, the ear, and the conspiracy theories

There are other claims I have seen circulating about Trump that I deliberately will not present as established fact.

I have seen claims that particular shootings were false-flag operations.

I have seen claims that Trump’s ear injury was somehow fabricated or that his ear “grew back.”

I have seen countless theories about secret operations, staged events and conspiracies.

Some of these theories may be emotionally satisfying to people who already despise Trump.

That isn’t good enough for me.

I don’t know that those things happened.

So I won’t tell you that they did.

If evidence emerges, I will examine it.

Until then, those claims belong in the category of allegation and speculation—not fact.

And frankly, Donald Trump’s actual record is sufficiently horrifying that I don’t understand why his critics feel compelled to manufacture additional horrors.

We don’t need them.

The truth is already ugly enough.

So what do I actually believe?

I believe Donald Trump has demonstrated a pattern of behavior that I find morally repugnant.

I believe the criminal conviction matters.

I believe the E. Jean Carroll judgment matters.

I believe the extraordinary record of false and misleading statements matters.

I believe his documented relationship with Epstein warrants scrutiny without pretending that association is proof of a crime.

I believe the Mueller investigation deserves to be remembered for what it actually found rather than whatever political mythology Trump would prefer.

I believe the Reflecting Pool episode demonstrates the danger of turning presidential accusations into accepted facts before the evidence is established.

I believe the suspiciously timed market trades surrounding major policy announcements deserve serious investigation.

I believe the Iran war deserves relentless scrutiny, particularly when the president who promised peace is now contemplating prolonged military involvement and talking openly about controlling another country’s oil.

And I believe that stripping healthcare from millions of people is not merely a budgetary adjustment.

It is a human decision.

A moral decision.

A political decision.

And I am furious about it.

I am furious because I think we have become numb.

Numb to the lies.

Numb to the cruelty.

Numb to the scandals.

Numb to the insults.

Numb to the convictions.

Numb to the constant assault on reality.

Numb to the idea that a president can say something demonstrably false at breakfast, contradict himself by lunch, attack whoever points it out by dinner, and wake up the next morning with everyone expected to pretend that yesterday never happened.

I am not numb.

And I don’t intend to become numb.

I don’t think Donald Trump is merely an asshole.

That word is too small.

An asshole is somebody who cuts you off in traffic.

An asshole is somebody who treats a waiter badly.

An asshole is somebody who ruins Thanksgiving.

There are words for political disagreement.

There are words for incompetence.

There are words for narcissism.

And then there are words for conduct that I believe crosses a much darker moral line.

Heinous is the word I choose.

Not because I think an insult is an argument.

It isn’t.

The argument is the record.

The insult is my reaction to it.

And after watching that record accumulate for years, I no longer feel any obligation to disguise my disgust in polite language simply because the person I am criticizing happens to occupy the most powerful office in the United States.

I can be furious.

I can be vulgar.

I can be merciless.

But I will also be precise.

Because I don’t need to lie about Donald Trump.

He has given his critics more than enough material without our help.
