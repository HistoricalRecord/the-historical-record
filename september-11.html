The Memorial

The people killed in the attacks are remembered individually by name.

2,977 lives

The September 11 attacks killed 2,977 people from around the world. They included office workers, firefighters, police officers, emergency medical personnel, airline crews, passengers, restaurant workers, maintenance workers, visitors and people who happened to be where the attacks occurred.

The National September 11 Memorial & Museum preserves the names and historical record associated with those killed. The Memorial itself contains 2,983 names because the six people killed in the February 26, 1993 World Trade Center bombing are also inscribed there.

The FAA orders all civilian aircraft in the United States to land at the nearest suitable airport.

Rescue, Recovery and Health

The September 11 story continued long after the buildings fell.

The people who went into the ruins

Thousands of people worked or volunteered at the World Trade Center site and related locations after the attacks. They included firefighters, police officers, paramedics, construction workers, ironworkers, sanitation workers, engineers, medical personnel, volunteers, cleaners, laborers and many others.

The aftermath continued for decades.
Dust and debris exposure became a major long-term public-health issue. The World Trade Center Health Program provides medical monitoring and treatment for eligible responders and survivors with certified 9/11-related conditions.

Rescue & Recovery

Searching for survivors and remains, stabilizing structures, removing debris, cleaning buildings and supporting the recovery operation.

Long-Term Illness

Respiratory conditions, cancers and other physical and mental health conditions have been associated with 9/11 exposure.

Documentation

Work location, duties, dates and exposure history became important parts of later health and compensation systems.

Firsthand Testimony

Oral histories preserve experiences that cannot be reconstructed from official documents alone.

Immigrant Workers

Immigrants were part of the recovery workforce at Ground Zero.

Workers who helped clear the ruins

The recovery operation depended on a large and diverse workforce. Immigrants, including undocumented workers, performed cleaning, demolition, debris-removal and related jobs around Ground Zero.

Immigration status could complicate access to information, employment records, health care and compensation systems. Fear, informal employment arrangements and documentation problems created additional barriers for some workers.

Immigrant labor Immigrants performed important recovery and cleanup work.
Documentation Employment and exposure records could be difficult to establish.
Health Workers experienced long-term health consequences associated with exposure.
