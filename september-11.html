September 11, 2001

Remembering the people, preserving the voices, examining the evidence, and documenting the lives touched by September 11 and its aftermath.

A Place for the Historical Record

This page is intended to bring together the human stories and historical record of September 11, 2001 in one place. It preserves the names and memory of those killed, the experiences of survivors and responders, the work of people who came to the site afterward, contemporary media, oral histories, photographs, government records, and the long aftermath of the attacks.

The Memorial

Names first. People before statistics.

2,977 lives

The September 11 attacks killed 2,977 people from 90 nations. The victims included office workers, firefighters, police officers, emergency medical personnel, airline crews, passengers, restaurant workers, maintenance workers, visitors, and people who happened to be where the attacks occurred.

The National September 11 Memorial & Museum preserves their names, portraits, personal effects, biographies, remembrances, and oral histories. The Memorial's official name directory provides the authoritative spelling and placement of every name.

Memorial principle: A name is not a statistic. Where reliable biographical material exists, this archive should give visitors a way to encounter the person behind it.

September 11: A Chronological Record

A concise chronology to orient the deeper source material.

Morning

Four commercial aircraft are hijacked

Nineteen al-Qaeda terrorists hijacked four commercial aircraft. American Airlines Flight 11 and United Airlines Flight 175 were flown toward the World Trade Center. American Airlines Flight 77 was flown toward the Pentagon. United Airlines Flight 93 crashed in Pennsylvania after passengers and crew attempted to regain control of the aircraft.

World Trade Center

The Twin Towers are struck

The attacks on the World Trade Center created an unfolding emergency involving thousands of civilians, firefighters, police officers, emergency medical personnel, workers and people attempting to escape.

Pentagon

Flight 77 strikes the Pentagon

American Airlines Flight 77 struck the Pentagon in Arlington, Virginia, killing people aboard the aircraft and inside the building.

Pennsylvania

Flight 93 crashes near Shanksville

Passengers and crew on Flight 93 learned through telephone calls that other hijacked aircraft had struck targets. They attempted to retake the aircraft before it crashed in an open field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania.

Later that morning

The World Trade Center towers collapse

The North and South Towers ultimately collapsed after the aircraft impacts and resulting fires severely damaged the structures.

The Rescue, Recovery and Cleanup

The story continued long after September 11.

The people who went into the ruins

Thousands of people worked or volunteered at the World Trade Center site and related locations after the attacks. They included firefighters, police officers, paramedics, construction workers, ironworkers, demolition specialists, sanitation workers, engineers, medical personnel, volunteers and many others.

The historical record also includes people whose work was less visible: cleaners, food-service workers, laborers, drivers and subcontracted workers who participated in the enormous cleanup operation.

The aftermath was not over when the fires went out.
Dust and debris exposure became a major long-term public-health issue. The federal World Trade Center Health Program now provides medical monitoring and treatment for certified 9/11-related conditions to eligible responders and survivors.

Rescue & Recovery

The work at Ground Zero included searching for survivors and human remains, stabilizing structures, removing debris, cleaning buildings, transporting material and supporting the enormous recovery operation.

Long-Term Illness

The WTC Health Program identifies and treats certified conditions associated with 9/11 exposure, including respiratory conditions, cancers and other physical and mental health conditions.

Documentation Matters

Eligibility systems have historically required evidence concerning where a person worked, what they did, when they were there and how long they worked at a covered location.

The Human Record

Oral histories are particularly important because official records can establish what happened while first-person testimony preserves what the experience felt like.

The Immigrant Workers of Ground Zero

A part of the September 11 story that deserves to remain visible.

They came to clean up the ruins too.

The recovery operation depended on a large and diverse workforce. Among those workers were immigrants, including undocumented workers, who performed cleaning, demolition, debris-removal and related jobs around Ground Zero.

Their immigration status could make an already dangerous situation more complicated. Some workers feared government authorities or retaliation by employers. Some had informal employment arrangements and limited documentation. Research also found substantial barriers to health care, workers' compensation and other systems intended to assist people injured by the work.

This should not be simplified into the claim that no health care or compensation ever existed. The Victim Compensation Fund did not categorically exclude applicants because of immigration status, and the World Trade Center Health Program now serves eligible responders and survivors. The historical problem was that many immigrant workers faced barriers to learning about, documenting eligibility for, and safely accessing these systems.

Immigrant labor Immigrants performed important rescue, recovery, cleaning and rebuilding work after the attacks.
Documentation barriers Informal employment and fear could make it difficult for workers to document where and when they had worked.
Health consequences Studies documented serious health concerns among immigrant workers, including respiratory illness and difficulties obtaining care.

Research basis: U.S. Environmental Protection Agency evaluation of the response to the World Trade Center collapse; peer-reviewed research on immigrant rescue and recovery workers; National Public Radio reporting; and current federal eligibility information.

How to Use This Archive

A path for readers who want to go deeper.

1

Begin with the names

Start with the Memorial directory. Remember that the people killed were individuals with families, jobs, friendships, ambitions and ordinary lives.

2

Listen to people

Oral histories preserve experiences that cannot be reduced to a chronology or government report.

3

Examine photographs

Study images as historical documents. Identify who created them, when, where and under what archival or copyright conditions.

4

Read primary records

Use the Commission records, FAA records, government reports and declassified documents to establish chronology and institutional decision-making.

5

Study the aftermath

September 11 did not end on September 11. The recovery operation, illnesses, compensation systems, immigration questions, wars, memorialization and family grief continued for decades.

6

Compare sources

When sources disagree, preserve the disagreement and identify the evidence rather than silently choosing whichever account is most convenient.

Archival Principles

Accuracy comes before completeness. A historical archive should never fill an empty space with an invented fact, photograph, quotation, biography or attribution.

Sources remain attached to their evidence. When a photograph, recording, document or quotation comes from another institution, this archive identifies the institution and links back to the original collection.

Victims are represented as people. Names, photographs, biographies and family remembrances should be treated with dignity and context.

Historical uncertainty is allowed. If researchers do not know something, the archive should say that it is unknown rather than inventing an answer.

Graphic material requires context. Images of death, destruction and human remains should never be used merely for shock value. Where such material is included elsewhere in the archive, it should be clearly identified and presented for historical purposes.
